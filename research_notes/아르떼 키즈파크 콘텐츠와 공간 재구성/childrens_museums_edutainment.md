## Q1. How leading US children's museums define concept, organize zones, size, and serve 5–10s vs toddlers

### Takeaway
Leading US children's museums are built around a "play is learning" / open-ended play philosophy and organize space into roughly 8–20 themed permanent galleries. Recurring archetypes are: water play, a large vertical climbing structure as an architectural icon, role-play "kid city" towns, construction/building zones, maker/tinkering labs, culture/immersion rooms, outdoor nature play, and a gated birth–3/toddler zone. Toddler zones are physically separated (e.g., Please Touch has four areas for ages 3 and under; MCM's "Habitot"). 5–10s are served mainly by climbing, role-play cities with "jobs"/economies, physics and engineering tinkering, and maker labs. Facility sizes run from about 40,000 sq ft (Thinkery) to about 470,000+ sq ft (Indianapolis), and gallery space typically falls between 16,000 and 65,000 sq ft.

### Inferences
- The content archetypes that recur at every leading museum map well onto AKP's three axes:
  - **Repetitive play:** water tables and fountains, ball and air systems, physics tracks (Physics Forest).
  - **Creation:** maker labs (Inventing Lab), paper-art stations, construction zones.
  - **Exploration:** climbing structures (Scramble, Hawk's Nest, Crane), immersive cultural houses.
- A tall, visible climbing structure serves as both a brand icon and an anchor for 5–10s (MCM's 4-story Scramble, Kidspace climbing tower). AKP's planned physical-play and rides could take this role as a media-augmented "vertical icon."
- Role-play economies (Kidtropolis, KidZania) are the dominant 5–10 format at US museums, but they rely on currency or job-loop systems, which are close to "collection mechanics." AKP's no-collection rule suggests open-ended role-play without tokens or scoring.
- Every major museum gates an under-3 area. AKP's "infants with parents" segment should get a physically separated, calmer, low-stimulus zone; four separate toddler areas at Please Touch show the scale of the commitment.

- Portland Children's Museum status (it announced closure in 2023 and later sought re-opening) was **not checked**.
- No 2025–2026 attendance figures were retrieved for any museum except Indianapolis's 2019 record.

## Q2. Commercial edutainment comparables: KidZania, LEGO(LAND) Discovery Center, Peppa Pig, Sesame Street

### Takeaway
In the US, commercial "edutainment" is dominated by small-box, IP-driven mall attractions (about 30,000–35,000 sq ft LEGO Discovery Centers; smaller Peppa Pig and Sesame Street play centers). The LEGO model combines a toddler zone, building zones, a 4D cinema, a model "Miniland," and 1–2 small rides, with birthday-party rooms as a key revenue stream. KidZania has only one US site (Frisco, TX). Its announced multi-city US roll-out has not materialized.

## Q3. Membership share of attendance and visits per member

### Takeaway
Members are a large and loyal share of children's museum attendance. Indianapolis reported about 49% member visits in 2019, while Memphis was about 15% in FY2012. ACM data show museums price memberships to break even at about 2.6–3.9 visits for a family of four, and assume about 4 visits per year from "value-seeking" members. Party-room discounts and member-only events are the most common membership perks.

### Cited Findings
- **ACM Trends #6.1 (Nov 2022, Knology), 90 museums, 30 per size category, family of four:**
  - Average admission: small $29.80, medium $40.60, large $59.20, all $43.19.
  - Average yearly membership: $112, $138, $151, $134.28 respectively.
  - Visits needed to equal a membership: 3.88 (small), 3.52 (medium), 2.62 (large), 3.33 (all).
  — [ACM Trends 6.1 PDF](https://childrensmuseums.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/6.1-Value-Pricing-and-the-Cultivation-of-Public-Trust.pdf)
- ACM: "museums anticipate four visits a year by their value-seeking members, setting prices that justify at least four or more visits in a membership year." — [ACM Trends 6.1](https://childrensmuseums.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/6.1-Value-Pricing-and-the-Cultivation-of-Public-Trust.pdf)
- Common membership benefits in the ACM sample: discounts on rentals or birthday parties (n=60 of 90), member-only events (n=53), ACM reciprocal admission (n=24), ASTC reciprocal (n=13). All large museums offered extra benefits. A space-rental discount was the only benefit significantly associated with higher membership prices. — [ACM Trends 6.1](https://childrensmuseums.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/6.1-Value-Pricing-and-the-Cultivation-of-Public-Trust.pdf)
- In a 51-museum subset, the median number of memberships purchased per year was 2,200–3,000 in FY2016–FY2019. It fell to 968 (FY2020) and 1,188 (FY2021), and the member share of visitors fell about 6% in 2021. The report does not print the absolute member-share percentage. — [ACM Trends 6.1](https://childrensmuseums.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/6.1-Value-Pricing-and-the-Cultivation-of-Public-Trust.pdf)
- ACM separates "value-seeking" members (who buy to save on frequent visits) from "affinity" members (who buy to support the museum, buy as gifts, or for perks such as birthday discounts, priority registration and exclusive programs). — [ACM Trends 6.1](https://childrensmuseums.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/6.1-Value-Pricing-and-the-Cultivation-of-Public-Trust.pdf)
- **Indianapolis 2019:** 1,318,915 total visits, of which 651,173 were member visits, a record; member households also hit a record of 36,110. Derived figures (author's calculations): about 49% of visits by members and about 18 member visits per household per year, where member visits count individuals rather than family visits. — [Inside Indiana Business](https://www.insideindianabusiness.com/articles/childrens-museum-sets-annual-attendance-record)
- **Children's Museum of Memphis FY2012:** 25,036 member visits out of 167,895 total, about 14.9% (calculated). **[from search snippet of annual report; not opened]** — [CMOM Annual Report 2012 (issuu)](https://issuu.com/cmom/docs/annual_report_2012)
- **Madison Children's Museum (2019):** over 3,400 families joined through an access-membership program and visited more than 26,000 times, about 7–8 visits per family (calculated). **[snippet]** — [MCM Annual Report 2019 (issuu)](https://issuu.com/madisonchildrensmuseum/docs/mcm-annual-report-2019)
- ACM's Trends Data Hub benchmarks museums using 990s, membership surveys and membership renewals. — [ACM Data Hub](https://datahub.childrensmuseums.org/about)

### Inferences
- If AKP behaves like a large children's museum, an annual pass priced at about 2.5–3.5× a family single-visit ticket is consistent with US norms. Party-room discounts are a standard perk that also lifts pass value.
- Member share varies widely, from about 15% to about 49%. Large flagship venues with strong rotating programming (Indianapolis) reach about half of attendance from members. Without collection mechanics or RFID, AKP's repeat-visit engine has to come from passes plus content rotation and programming (see Q4).

### Gaps
- No field-wide median for "members as % of attendance" was published in the ACM report retrieved. AAM and IMLS sources were not reached.
- Commercial operators (LEGO, KidZania) do not publish annual-pass share.

## Q4. Evidence on what drives dwell time and repeat visits (water, building, climbing, open-ended)

### Takeaway
The published evidence is thin and mostly from single-site evaluations. It does consistently show that average time at a single exhibit is about one minute (well aligned with AKP's 1–3 minute sessions), that only about 15% of children return to an exhibit they already used, and that climbing structures and water elements earn the longest dwell and most repeat stops in early-childhood areas. At the institution level, repeat visitation is driven by memberships, rotating or licensed blockbuster exhibits, and programs.

### Cited Findings
- **Exploratorium-led observational study (Dancstep & Gutwill, 2019):** 906 children aged 8–13 at 301 STEM exhibits in three science centers. Mean time per exhibit was 62.5 s (SD 40.6; range 9.2–273.9 s). 15% of children returned to an exhibit already used (SD 9%; range 0–60%). 22% of children used the average exhibit. Low-density spaces showed greater holding time, and walls around exhibits have been linked to longer visits (citing Adamson 2008; Allen 2004). — [InformalScience summary](https://informalscience.org/how-do-children-engage-stem-museum-exhibits-results-large-observational-study/)
- **"Tot Spot" early-childhood exhibit tracking study:** families stayed about 27 minutes on average versus a typical exhibit average of about 15 minutes, spending somewhat more time in the water room than the forest room. Climbing Structure and Waterbeds were where people spent the most time. **[UNVERIFIED: from search snippet; exact source document is likely one of these but not confirmed]** — [informalscience report_234](https://archive.informalscience.org/sites/default/files/report_234.PDF) (503 on fetch); [UW ResearchWorks](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/3bd9602b-3e12-4761-955a-821c08c5e5e2)
- **Melbourne Museum children's gallery study:** the Upper Climbing Net ranked first in number of repeat visitors and first in stops at an exhibit. Water and sand pits had among the lowest caregiver interaction. **[UNVERIFIED: search snippet; source URL not confirmed, possibly core.ac.uk/download/212970106.pdf]** — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/212970106.pdf)
- **University of Washington / Imagine Children's Museum evaluation:** longer and deeper engagement was associated with exhibits offering clear goals, collaborative opportunities and appropriate challenge. **[snippet]** — [UW: Children's Engagement in New and Old Exhibits](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/3bd9602b-3e12-4761-955a-821c08c5e5e2)
- **Rotating and licensed exhibits as repeat drivers:** Indianapolis attributed a membership-household record to the Doc McStuffins exhibit and promoted Wild Weather and Barbie for 2020. — [Inside Indiana Business](https://www.insideindianabusiness.com/articles/childrens-museum-sets-annual-attendance-record)
- **Water play is persistent across flagships:** Chicago reinvested to replace WaterWays with Water City, and Thinkery's design was publicized as "you will get wet." — [Contractor Mag](https://www.contractormag.com/around-the-web/article/21176916/chicago-childrens-museum-launches-water-city); [Architect's Newspaper](https://www.archpaper.com/2014/03/you-will-get-wet/)

### Inferences
- A one-minute per-exhibit norm validates AKP's 1–3 minute session design. Zone-level dwell, however, comes from clustering many short loops in an open-ended environment (Tot Spot: 27 minutes per zone). AKP should design zones as bundles of short loops rather than single linear experiences.
- A 15% exhibit return rate is low. Open-ended, physical and changeable content (climbing, water, building) attracts repeat stops more than goal-ended screens. For AKP, media interactions whose output changes each time (generative visuals responding to body, water or drawing) are the digital analogue of open-ended play.
- With no collection or RFID loop, AKP's repeat-visit levers are:
  - an annual pass priced at about 3 visits;
  - seasonal content swaps (the media art equivalent of traveling exhibits);
  - a programs calendar (Live Atelier classes, member-only hours);
  - party-room member discounts.

### Gaps
- No rigorous study directly links water or building exhibits to institution-level repeat visitation. Evidence is exhibit-level and single-site.
- No published data was found on dwell time in digital or immersive kids' venues (teamLab, d'strict) in comparison with physical children's museums.
- The 2010 RK&A Please Touch audience study (visit length, member behavior) could not be accessed (HTTP 503).
